Understanding Small Lies in Childhood

Small untruths often hide bigger fears, not bad intent.

An illustrative, composite scenario — not a real named incident. Educational content only; not medical or legal advice. For diagnosis or support, consult qualified professionals.

"Sam" told his teacher he finished his drawing when he hadn’t, worried about getting in trouble for taking too long.

At home, "Sam" said he brushed his teeth, though he skipped it, afraid his parents would scold him for being forgetful.

Behaviour is communication

Children like "Sam" are not trying to be bad but often avoid sharing the truth to escape fear or disappointment.

What was likely really going on

⏳

Fear of Consequences

Kids may lie about small things to avoid punishment or negative reactions from adults.

🔊

Need for Control

Telling a small untruth can feel like a way for a child to gain safety or control when unsure of outcomes.

🧠

Developmental Understanding

Young children are still learning about honesty and may not fully grasp social expectations around truth-telling.

What could have helped

🎭

Reggio Emilia: Creating Trusting Environments

Providing a welcoming space that values the child’s perspective helps children feel safe to express their true thoughts.

🧺

Montessori: Freedom Within Limits

Offering choices and clear boundaries allows children to practice honesty while feeling empowered and supported.

💛

Early-childhood practice: Regulate First, Teach Second

Calming a child’s emotions first creates readiness for gentle teaching about honesty without triggering fear.

Try this at home & school

  • Notice the feelings behind small untruths before reacting.
  • Offer calm responses that invite honesty rather than punishment.
  • Use consistent, clear rules and gentle reminders about truthfulness.
  • Create moments to connect and listen without judgment.
  • Model honesty in everyday conversations and admit your own mistakes.
