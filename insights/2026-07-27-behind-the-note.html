Behind the note that came home

A child with ADHD is flagged for bad words and a shove at recess. The note says what happened — never why. Here's what was likely going on, and what could have helped.

An illustrative, composite scenario — not a real named incident. Educational content only; not medical or legal advice. For diagnosis or support, consult qualified professionals.

Ten-year-old “Sam” has mild ADHD. On Thursday, a note comes home: during recess he swore and shoved another child. The note is factual and calm — it lists the behaviour, the consequence, and asks parents to “have a conversation at home.”

Everything in that note is about what happened. Nothing in it asks why. And in most of these moments, the why is the whole story.

Behaviour is communication

In early-childhood practice we start simple: a child’s behaviour is a message, not a character flaw. A ten-year-old who swears and shoves is rarely “being bad” — he’s usually a child whose regulation ran out before his words could catch up.

What was likely really going on

⏳

Transition overload

Moving from a structured lesson to loud, fast, unstructured recess is one of the hardest shifts of the day for an ADHD brain.

🔊

Sensory flood

Noise, movement and jostling can tip a child from managing to overwhelmed in seconds — the shove is often a body reacting, not a plan.

🧠

Depleted self-control

By late morning the effort of holding it together all day is spent. The swearing is a regulation tank hitting empty.

What could have helped

🎭

Reggio Emilia: the environment as teacher

See the child as capable and adjust the setting. A calmer entry to recess, a quiet corner to choose, a trusted adult nearby — the space does some of the regulating.

🧺

Montessori: freedom within limits

Predictable structure and real choices lower the pressure. A known routine into recess, with the option to start slow, gives control back before overwhelm builds.

💛

Early-childhood practice: regulate first, teach second

An overwhelmed child can’t problem-solve. Co-regulate first — calm body, calm voice — then, later, teach the words and the repair.

Try this at home & school

  • Ask “what happened right before?” before deciding what the behaviour meant.
  • Build a gentler on-ramp to recess: a two-minute warning, a job, or a calmer start.
  • Offer a pre-agreed exit — a quiet spot or a check-in adult — before overwhelm peaks.
  • Teach the words for “I’m done” during calm moments, not in the heat of it.
  • Repair after regulating: name it, make it right, move on — without shame.

Every behaviour has a reason.
